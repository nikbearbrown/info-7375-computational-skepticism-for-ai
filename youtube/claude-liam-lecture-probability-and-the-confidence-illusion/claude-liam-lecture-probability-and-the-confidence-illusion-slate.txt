A diagnostic that tells you whether a model's numbers mean what they say and a warning about the one situation where averages stop working. Probability is a degree of belief. Most engineers come up Boolean, a bit is one or zero, a test passes or fails. Even error bars arrive as a small wobble around a yes or no answer. Probability is a different animal. Its output is not true or false. It is a degree of belief, a number from zero to one saying how confident to be given what you currently know. So two engineers can look at the same evidence land on different probabilities in both reason correctly. They walked in with different priors, different starting beliefs. Probability is always conditional on what you know. To compute with it you need three objects, a sample space, every possible outcome for a die one through six, an event, any subset like the even faces two, four, and six, and a measure that assigns each event a number. That measure must obey three axioms, never negative. The whole sample space has probability one because something has to happen, and for events that cannot both occur, probabilities add. Two consequences follow at once. Nothing happening has probability zero, and the chance that an event does not happen is one minus the chance that it does. Everything else here is built from these rules. There are two natural ways to assign the numbers. Classical when outcomes are equally likely, a bag holds one ball each of five colors, so drawing yellow is one in five. Frequentist for repeatable experiments, toss a coin 20 times and seven heads is a frequency of 720th. Keep tossing, and if the process is stable, that frequency settles toward a limit. Neither answers what a deployed system faces every minute. Given this output right now, how should my belief about this case change? That is Bayesian probability. Start with a prior C evidence update to a posterior. Use all three and do not get tribal. Conditioning on the chauhne. Conditioning is the engine, learning that B happened shrinks the world to B. The probability of a given B is the part of B that also lies in A, the overlap divided by B. A flagged case arrives 30 over 76, about 39.5%, lower than the 45%, you would guess with no flag at all. While lower if flag and acuity were unrelated, both would show up together 34.2% of the time. They show up 30%. In this log, the flag goes with high acuity less than chance. The formula does the work intuition will not. Rearrange the definition, and you get the multiplication rule. The chance that both happen is the chance of the first, times the chance of the second given the first, chain it, and you can reason about anything that happens in sequence. So 101 people test positive and one of them is sick, one in a hundred and one, about 1% not 99. You answered the right question in the wrong direction. 99% is the chance of a positive given the disease. You needed the chance of the disease given a positive, getting from one to the other takes the base rate. Base runs backward. That conversion is base theorem. The posterior is the prior times the likelihood divided by everything that could produce a positive. The total probability theorem run the other way. Run it prior one in 10,000 hit rate, 0.99 false alarm rate, 0.01. The answer is exactly one in a hundred and two. The simple count said one in a hundred and one because it let the test catch the sick person for certain. The positive result did real work. It raised the probability about 98 times, but the prior was so low that the posterior is still small. Both facts matter and neither shows if you read only the accuracy. One honest caution, 99% accurate was doing double duty as the rate for catching the sick and as the rate for clearing the healthy. This structure appears wherever the thing you hunt is rare, fraud, intrusion, screening, moderation, an excellent detector then raises mostly false alarms. Only two escapes exist, raise the base rate or accept more missed cases. Operators check the first alerts, false, the next few, false. If you cannot answer that, you do not yet trust the record, you just have not noticed. Calibration. Calibration, a model is calibrated when it stated probabilities match what happens. Say 70% on 1,000 cases and about 700 should turn out positive. If 400 do, it is overconfident, 900 underconfident. Put stated confidence across an actual frequency up. A calibrated model hugs the diagonal. A miscalibrated one peels away and the shape of the peel says how it is wrong. Geo plus sun and wineberger at ICML 2017 found that modern neural networks, unlike those of a decade earlier, are poorly calibrated and that depth, width, weight, decay, and batch normalization all affected. Their cheap fix is temperature scaling. Divided the logits, the scores before the softmax by one learned number T, then applied the softmax. With T above one, confidence softens toward honesty. We fit T on held out data 2.19, the dot slide onto the diagonal. The top bin now states 94% in his right 94. Accuracy did not change and neither did a single decision. So confidence and correctness were separable all along. The number was decorative and temperature scaling is how you undecorated, but it only fixes average calibration on the data you fitted to. If the deployment world drifts from that calibration set, the honest number goes stale silently, on the tail, on the novel case, on the day the world moves. Honest confidence bought cheaply on the data you have, paid for in silence on the data you do not. So never trust a stated probability without its calibration curve on data from the actual deployment and decide in advance what would make you reject the number. Without that, you are judging a score by how confident it sounds. Calibration applies to you too. Ask people for 90% intervals and the truth falls inside far less often than 90. Reviews of this research put it between 30% and 60% about half. Run the experiment on yourself. When averages stop settling, the central limit theorem says sums of many independent variables with finite variance look like a bell curve, which is why averages are useful. Two requirements hide inside, independence and finite variance, a finite spread. Break either and the failure is quiet. Independence breaks in networks and in time series that remember. Finite variance breaks in heavy tailed distributions where extreme events are too common for averaging to dampen them. Watch the running average of 5,000 draws. From a bell curve, it settles near zero. From a Cauchy distribution, it never does. One draw minus 43,000 moved it by almost 12 and at 5,000 it sat at minus 6.8. For deployment, the question is the distribution of the cost of being wrong. Suppose with made up numbers, a security alert system wastes $10 on each false alarm, but a missed breach costs $500,000. An average loss metric will look beautiful and answer the wrong question because the rare catastrophic case is the only one that matters. Use tail-aware metrics, worst case analysis and adversarial inputs that surface catastrophes in testing. The honest admission, there is no clean way to tell before the catastrophe that a loss is heavy tailed. Estimating tails from a sample is mathematically impolite. The working rule, a heuristic and not a calculation. Assume heavy tails when errors can harm people. For questions, most failures of confidence are not failures of competence. They are failures of interpretation, intuition that forgets the prior, trusts the stated number, averages over a heavy tail and assumes the world has not changed. So ask four questions in order. What is the base rate? Is the model calibrated on data from this deployment? What is the distribution of the cost of being wrong and what has changed since training?
