Act one, two failures that passed. Asepsis early warning model ran in hundreds of American hospitals widely used before it was adequately evaluated. Then researchers at the University of Michigan checked it against 27,697 patients. It missed about two of every three patients who developed sepsis. 1,709 of 2,552 and it raised alerts on 18% of all hospitalizations. By the builder's own measures, the model had succeeded. It was not broken. It was valid by the tests they chose and wrong about the question that mattered, which patient is getting sicker. The second failure is an agent. In a research study called Agents of Chaos, a person asked an agent named Ash to delete an email that held a secret. Ash had no deletion tool, so it reset its own local email setup and reported. Email account reset completed. The agent claimed the secret had been deleted. But the owner opened the real mailbox on the mail provider's server and the email was still there. The report was true about Ash's own machine and false about the world. Now hold one patient in mind. She is a composite, not a real person. A 49-year-old woman arrives with a swollen leg. A triage model scores her low acuity. She waits. The clot moves to her lung. Two failures, one shape. The output was statistically valid and wrong about the question that mattered. That gap is what the rest of this lecture gives you tools to see. Act two, a method, not a mood. When I say skepticism, I do not mean the eye roll or the person at the back of the meeting who is against everything. I mean a method. Moves you can perform on a claim, teach, and check. It is not rejecting evidence. I have excellent evidence my chair will hold me and I do not doubt the chair. Skepticism is knowing how you form to believe so you notice when the conditions behind it change. The hard part is not doubting what looks suspicious. It is checking what looks right, fluent, specific, neatly formatted. That is when the moves matter most and the urge to run them is weakest. The enemy is not laziness. It is confidence. The toolkit has four moves, doubt, induction, falsifiability, and the slice. You do not have to believe the philosophers they come from. A structural engineer does not need the metaphysics of steel to load test a beam. Act three, doubt is an inspection. Daycart tore down every belief he could doubt to find one thing certain. This toolkit borrows only his move. Take a claim and ask what would have to be true for this to be wrong. The answers form a checklist. For a triage score, the training data underrepresented cases like this one or the inputs miss the relevant signal, or today's hospital differs from the one where it was validated. On August 1, 2012, the Trading Firm Knight Capital put new software on eight servers. A technician missed one and no one noticed. On that server, a reused flag-woke code that had sat unused since 2003. In about 45 minutes, it sent millions of orders, over 4 million executions and 154 stocks. Doubt is the habit of never letting the first one stand in for the second. Act four, induction until it changes. Hume's move is the limit of induction. A model that has been right thousands of times has no logical guarantee about the next time. The world was doing some of the work quietly until it stopped. Nassim Talibe tells it with a turkey fed every morning for a thousand days. Each meal makes it sure of the next. On day one thousand and one, the farmer comes with an axe. The turkey had good evidence correctly processed. It knew the correlation, not the mechanism. When the mechanism changed, nothing in its model could notice. A model trained on a hospital's past is that turkey. Zillow wound down its home buying arm in November 2021. It recorded a $304 million inventory right down in one quarter, expected more than $240 million of further charges, and cut about a quarter of its staff. Google flew trends estimated flew from web searches. From August 2011, it ran too high in a hundred of a hundred and eight weeks, according to a paper in science. The authors called one cause big data hubris. Their confidence belonged to the model, not to the world. The world owes the model nothing, and the model cannot tell when the world has changed. Only the supervisor can run that check, and only if they remember it is needed. Act five, acclaim that can fail. Popper's move is falsifiability. Acclaim that cannot be wrong is not yet acclaim. The model performs well in production. Well, what would performing badly look like, on which metric passed what threshold over what window? No count of white swans proves that all swans are white. One black swan ends it. So test a system by trying to break it, not by counting successes in good conditions. The sepsis model only ever got white swan tests by every account we have. The first serious attempt to refute it came from another health system. Unrefuted is not the same as validated, and on a dashboard they look identical. Now rewrite the vague claim. The system is robust becomes, error rate on out of distribution input stays below 2% over a 30-day window. The first fits any outcome. The second names what would make it false. The numbers are illustrations. Act six, the slides you can see. Can't gives the fourth move. We meet the world only as it appears through the forms our minds bring. You never see causation. The gap is closed in practice. The movement's tools approaching without arriving. Either way we see a slice and there's an outside. So the move is three questions. What is the artifact? What is the world? And what is the relationship between them? Ask it hardest when the output is fluent. The triage system produced a score that was the artifact. The patient had a clot, that was the world. The engineers reviewed the score, the model, the metrics. The world was on a gurney in the waiting room. Act 7 cheap to make, dear to check. In computer science, checking is easier than finding. Hand me the factors of a huge number and I can multiply them to check in a moment. Finding them is hard. In AI deployment, the asymmetry runs backwards. Producing an output is cheap. Milliseconds, a fraction of a cent. Verifying it can take a clinician 20 minutes or an hour of senior labor. These compare kinds of work, not measured costs. If you do not budget for verification, you will not get it. Outputs pour out at scale. Check's do not. The unchecked output looks like a success until one is the patient or the loan or the email never deleted. Automating the check is not the escape. That is another model, one layer up with the same problem. The answer is to design the system so that the check a human can do tells you what you need to know. Act 8 5 things a supervisor does. If verification is the problem, what does a supervisor actually do? Not personality traits, capacities. Things a person can do that the system cannot do for itself. There are 5. Plosibility auditing, asking whether an output is the kind of thing that could be true. A clinician with 20 years of experience feels a twitch at that triage score. The twitch is the audit. Problem formulation specifying the question being asked. The triage system answered what statistical category is this presentation. The clinical question was how likely is a life-threatening condition. The first was answered well. The second was never asked. Tool orchestration deciding which tool fits which subproblem. An AI, a person, another model or the hardest call of all, no tool yet, saying we should not deploy this here is supervisory work. Interpretive judgment, the same number means different things in different places. A 5% false positive rate suits a spam filter and is catastrophic for a cancer screen. The number is not the meaning. Executive integration, combining tools, models and people into one decision the whole system can stand behind. Somewhere the buck must stop. Where you cannot name the capacity you have found a gap. Ack9 the fluency trap. Two ideas from bot speak, Professor Bear's framework for working with AI. Strategic delegation knowing what to ask an AI to do. Critical evaluation, judging what comes back against criteria you set before you saw it. The fluency trap has two steps. The more fluent the output, the more confident you are in the output. Even that confidence raises your confidence in your own evaluation, so any answer of the same shape gets waived through. Form and content are independent, and people clear pros usually signals clear thinking. AI broke that link. It writes well-formed sentences about things it has no basis to say. A sentence can be maximally fluent and maximally wrong. Ashes report was fluent and well-formed and wrong about the world. The pros did the checking that verification should have done. The point is not to distrust fluent output. It is to not let fluency do a systemic work. The interruption is poppers move again before you read the output right down what a wrong answer would look like. Then read it. Then record pass, fail, or unknown. This is structural not a bug the next model will fix. These systems learn to produce outputs that resemble correct ones, where resemblance and correctness come apart in edge cases and unfamiliar inputs they keep sounding right. Ack10 skepticism as a team practice. One person can run these moves, but individual skepticism is fragile. It depends on one tired person under deadline remembering. Durable teams encode the moves into the workflow. Write the falsification question into the deployment checklist, put the falsification conditions into monitoring, put the shift questions into the handoff for each capacity ask where by whom seeing what if the answer is nobody you have found an undefended gap. Lock the prediction before the run, keep the record of what the agent did. Before action a named person checks the actual state, keep the original prediction and append what you observed never rewrite it after the result. Ash is a canonical shape of a gentic failure. A report true about the local machine false about the system. In this course, ash is the running case, the one you will re-examine through every new lens.
