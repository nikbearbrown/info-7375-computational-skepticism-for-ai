## The content (the chapter's arc; one beat per step, cut anything that repeats)
Keep one cast: a coin, a bag of five balls (blue, green, red, yellow, black), a tally board, the Ω tray of outcome tiles, a 0-to-1 bar, a patient peg with a flag tile, a reader/engineer peg.
1. **Two natural ways to assign the numbers, and you've used both.** One line.
2. **Classical.** All outcomes equally likely: a fair coin, heads is one of two, so one half. The bag of five balls, draw without looking, yellow is one in five. The Laplace formula: outcomes in A over total outcomes. Requires symmetry.
3. **Frequentist.** Repeatable experiments: toss twenty times, seven heads, relative frequency seven twentieths; run it more and, provided the process is stable, the frequency converges to a limit we call the probability. The language of quality control, A/B tests, hypothesis tests on large samples. Can't assign a probability to a one-off event.
4. **Neither handles the deployed question.** Given this specific output right now, how should my belief about this specific case update? For that, the third interpretation.
5. **Bayesian.** A degree of belief updated by evidence: prior, evidence, posterior. Natural for one-offs: this transaction fraud? this patient the rare disease? this agent's task actually done? "Use all three. Use whichever fits the question. Do not get tribal about it." (The table's three rows are the chapter's; a `focus` or `table` card over them passes the card test only if the claim IS the row-by-row read; otherwise draw the three as three tiles.)
6. **Here is the engine.** Almost every calculation in the chapter, and most in the book, is a conditional probability. Partial information: the test came back positive; the transaction is over a thousand dollars; the image has a shadow lower left.
7. **The definition.** P(A given B) = P(A and B) over P(B), provided P(B) > 0. Numerator: both occur. Denominator: all of B. The fraction of B's probability that falls inside A.
8. **The picture: shrinking the sample space.** Once you know B happened, only outcomes in B are still possible; among those, what fraction are also in A? Draw the Ω tray with B's tiles lifted out into a smaller tray, A's tiles among them marked. (Your own drawing, not the chapter's Venn figure.)
9. **Why it's the engine.** When an agent reports "task complete," you don't want the raw rate at which such reports are true; you want the probability that this report is true given what you know: novel task, a tool that has failed before, an environment the agent never touched. Every audit in the book is a conditional probability wearing a different costume.
10. **The illustration.** The chapter's logged triage-support model: A = genuinely high-acuity, B = arrives with an abnormal lab flag. From the log: 76% carry the flag, 45% turn out high-acuity, 30% both. A flagged case arrives: 0.30 / 0.76 ≈ 0.395. About 39.5%, lower than the unconditional 45%. The flag lowered the estimate, because the flag is common and most flagged cases aren't serious. Label the numbers "the chapter's illustration."
11. **The trap the chapter circles.** A salient signal arrives, intuition reads it as bad news, the conditioning says otherwise. The formula does the work the intuition won't.
12. **One property to carry forward.** P(· given B) is itself a legitimate probability measure on the shrunken space B; it obeys all three axioms (tie back to the previous film in one line). You haven't learned a new tool, only moved into a smaller world.
13. **Your Turn idea:** take one signal your system treats as bad news (a flag, an alert, a failed check). Write A (the thing you actually care about) and B (the signal), then find or estimate the three numbers: how often B, how often A, how often both. Compute P(A given B) and compare it to P(A). Two checks: is P(A and B) really the overlap, not P(A) times P(B)? Did the number move the way your intuition said it would, and if not, which one do you trust?

## Extra examples (Bear, 2026-09-28, after the paste: "Add some examples to the frequentist, um, conditional probability film.")

Each is a constructed illustration with exact, derivable numbers; label them "example" on screen, never as data; keep the arithmetic on screen where the viewer can check it. Fold them into the beats above (a second motion on the same cast) or give each its own beat if Law 9 says so; never pad.
- **Frequentist, second example.** A die rolled 60 times shows a six 9 times: relative frequency 9/60 = 0.15; roll on and, if the die and the roll are stable, the frequency settles toward one sixth (≈ 0.167). Draw the tally board filling and the bar drifting toward the 1/6 mark. Optionally a deployment version: an agent run 200 times on the same task, 190 reports "complete" and verified: 190/200 = 0.95, a rate for that task under those conditions and nothing else (ties to the "stable process" caveat).
- **Conditional, simple example on the die (classical inside conditional).** A = the die shows even {2,4,6}; B = the die shows more than 3 {4,5,6}. P(A ∩ B) = P({4,6}) = 2/6; P(B) = 3/6; so P(A | B) = (2/6)/(3/6) = 2/3. Unconditionally P(A) = 1/2; knowing "more than 3" raised it. Draw the Ω tray shrinking to the three tiles {4,5,6} and two of them marked even. This is the "shrinking the sample space" picture with numbers the viewer can count.
- **Conditional, a signal that lowers the estimate (the chapter's shape, second instance).** A deck of 52 cards: A = the card is a king (4/52), B = the card is a face card (12/52, jack, queen, king). P(A | B) = (4/52)/(12/52) = 1/3, up from 4/52 ≈ 0.077. Then the reverse direction: A = the card is red (26/52), B = the card is a spade (13/52). P(A ∩ B) = 0, so P(A | B) = 0: knowing B rules A out entirely. Use one or both; the second shows conditioning can send a number to zero.
- **Conditional, the everyday deployment case.** Spam filter log (constructed): 40% of incoming mail has an external link, 10% is spam, 8% is both. Mail with a link arrives: P(spam | link) = 0.08/0.40 = 0.20, up from 0.10; the link doubles the estimate but 80% of linked mail is still fine. Contrast with the chapter's triage flag, where the same formula lowered the estimate: the formula doesn't know which way it will go until you put the numbers in.
