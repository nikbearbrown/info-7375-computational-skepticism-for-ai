## The content (the chapter's arc; one beat per step, cut anything that repeats)
Keep one cast: a bit (a small block that reads 1 or 0), a dial or bar from 0 to 1 (the degree of belief), a die, a patient peg, two engineer pegs, and a tray labelled Ω holding outcome tiles.
1. **The Boolean education.** A bit is one or zero; a program runs or doesn't; a test passes or fails; a theorem is proved or isn't. Even error bars come in as a small wobble around a yes-or-no answer.
2. **Probability is a different animal.** The output is a degree of belief: a number between zero and one that says how confident you should be, given what you currently know. Draw the bit becoming a dial.
3. **Two engineers, same evidence, different numbers, both correct.** Not mushy-relativistic; correctly. They walked in with different priors. Probability is always conditional on your information state. Draw two pegs looking at the same evidence card, each holding a different dial, each with a different prior tile behind them.
4. **Why it should bother you a little.** The Boolean instinct works in deterministic systems; it misleads you whenever you work from a model rather than the world, and every ML system, AI deployment and statistical inference is a model, not the world.
5. **Sample space and event, with the chapter's examples.** Ω for a die is {1,2,3,4,5,6}; for a patient, {sick, healthy}. An event is any subset: "even" is {2,4,6}. Draw the tray of six tiles and the three even ones lifting out.
6. **Axiom 1, nonnegativity.** P(A) ≥ 0. Probabilities are never negative. Simple example on the die: any event's bar starts at the floor, never below it.
7. **Axiom 2, normalization.** P(Ω) = 1. Something has to happen. The whole tray's bar fills to 1; the patient is sick or healthy, the two bars sum to the full height.
8. **Axiom 3, additivity.** For disjoint A and B, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B). Example: "even" {2,4,6} and "five" {5} can't both occur; their bars stack. (Show that it is for disjoint events; don't show a non-disjoint case as if it added.)
9. **That is the entire foundation.** Conditional probability, Bayes, the central limit theorem are all derived from these three. When you forget why a formula is true, come back here.
10. **Two consequences.** P(∅) = 0: nothing happening has probability zero. P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A): the die not showing even is 1 minus P(even). Draw the complement as the three tiles left in the tray.
11. **The close.** Every probability calculation in the book is a consequence of these three rules. And back to the opening: the number is your degree of belief given what you know, which is why two careful engineers can differ.
12. **Your Turn idea:** take one yes-or-no claim your system makes (a test passed, an agent succeeded, a patient is low-risk). Write its sample space, name the event, and put a number between 0 and 1 on it with one line saying what you know that the number is conditional on. Two checks: does your number and its complement sum to 1? Could a colleague with different information honestly write a different number, and can you say what they'd have to know?
