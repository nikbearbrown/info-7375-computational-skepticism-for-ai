Visualization under validation

Why a chart is an argument, how to show what is uncertain, and how to check it before it ships

Every chart a team ships is an argument, made through its axes, colors, layout and defaults before anyone reads a number. This deck covers how to choose a chart, how to show what is uncertain, how the common misleading moves work, and how to check a chart before it ships. It defines thirty-nine terms along the way, each tagged as explained in the course reading; the compact exam-term list marks this topic as not on the exam, and the terms also match the exam-term shortlist.

Conceptual illustration

The question

Same file, two dashboards

Source: Chapter 10, “Two dashboards built from one CSV”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

One validation result, a recommendation system tested on its users, holds three findings: it does well on the typical user, poorly on three subgroups, and it is overconfident at the top of its probability range. Hand that same file to two builders, one told to reassure a nervous partner and one told to get the partner asking the right questions. The partners leave with different beliefs about whether the system is safe, though not one number differs. The skill this deck trains is to have an AI build the chart, then catch and fix any misleading encoding before it ships.

Conceptual illustration

The central claim

A chart is a lens, not a window

Source: Chapter 10, “Why this chapter”, “The medium is the message”, “Why this chapter is load-bearing”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Engineers tend to treat a chart as a pane of glass through which data is simply seen. It is closer to a lens ground to a prescription: axis start, palette, aggregation and layout all bend what the reader takes away, and a prescription nobody chose is the plotting library's, tuned to look finished rather than honest. A chart that misstates sound findings is therefore a validation failure at the output layer, not a communication problem downstream. The machine can build the chart; auditing it before it ships stays with the person supervising.

Argument and channel

A dashboard argues before anyone reads a number

Source: Chapter 10, “Two dashboards built from one CSV”, “The medium is the message”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

A dashboard argues by its form before any number is read, which is McLuhan's point that the medium shapes the message. Headline-first says this number is the answer, and equal panels say no single number suffices. Counting what reaches the reader at first glance, the first layout shows one of three findings and the second shows all three, from the same file. When true numbers fail to deliver sound findings, the failure sits at the output layer.

Illustrative data

Argument and channel

Question family, then channel

Source: Chapter 10, “Step one: the question the chart is answering”; “Step two: the perception hierarchy”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

A chart answers one question, so state the question in a sentence, find its family among nine, and choose from the charts in that family. A trend in calibration over months is change over time, which calls for a line. Then choose the channel that carries the number: position on a common scale is read most accurately, then length, then angle, area and volume, with color weakest for quantity. Five nearly equal shares are easy to rank as dots on one scale and hard to rank as pie angles.

Simulation (seeded)

Showing uncertainty

Two faces of uncertainty

Source: Chapter 10, “Uncertainty has two faces”; “Showing the trust”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Uncertainty has two faces. Aleatoric uncertainty is chance variation in the data: thirty polls of one population give thirty different answers, and a poll gap of 0.8 points inside a margin of 2.95 is a tie. Epistemic uncertainty is doubt about whether the data measures the right thing, as when a count of news stories stands in for a count of events. Check that a proxy tracks the thing before drawing it, because a chart built on a bad proxy carries the full authority of a chart built on truth.

Simulation (seeded)

Showing uncertainty

Error bars, selective intervals, bands and the cone

Source: Chapter 10, “Showing the range”; “The deceptive visualization catalog”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

An error bar is a claim about spread, so every chart must say what the bar is, such as a 95 percent confidence interval. Showing intervals only where they flatter the argument is the most dishonest move, because the intervals exist: two estimates that overlap on the full chart look clearly separated once one interval is dropped. A band behind a line carries the amount of uncertainty in pale shading, so its width needs labels where it matters. A forecast cone is sized to hold about two thirds of past track errors, so about a third of outcomes land outside it, and it is not the size of the storm.

Computed

Showing uncertainty

Making probability countable

Source: Chapter 10, “Showing the range”; “Gigerenzer natural frequencies”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

A probability is easier to grasp as a count than as a percentage. Twenty dots with four beyond a line show a chance the reader can count, and a grid of 1,000 people with 9 true positives and 99 false positives shows that a positive result is real about 8.3 percent of the time. The uncertainty should also carry the same visual weight as the estimate, rather than a thin bar beside a bold number. Digits should stop where the margin stops: 0.85, not 0.847291.

Simulation (seeded)

Showing uncertainty

One sample, three summaries

Source: Chapter 10, “Showing the range”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Several techniques show a distribution, and each can hide something. A box plot reports the median and quartiles but can place its median in a gap between two clusters, where no case lies. A violin plot shows the shape, and a strip plot shows every case when there are few enough. A hypothetical outcome plot animates plausible draws so the reader feels the variability, which works on a screen and fails on paper.

Computed

Disclosure

Where the uncertainty goes

Source: Chapter 10, “Showing the trust”; “Hierarchy of disclosure”; Exercise 8; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

The hostile-reader test decides where uncertainty is shown: could a reader out to disagree reach a different conclusion by knowing it? If so, it goes prominently in the chart; if not, but a serious reader needs it, in a caption or footnote; if the conclusion is robust and the audience general, in a methodology appendix. A hierarchy orders the disclosure and does not hide it. The source belongs inside the chart too: the title is the question, the subtitle the answer, and a specific source line is the warrant.

Computed

Axes and summaries

Axes: start, match, scale, direction

Source: Chapter 10, “The deceptive visualization catalog”; “Four cases that teach the catalog”; Exercises 1, 3, 4; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Axes carry a chart's argument. Cutting a value axis at 91 makes a 2.2-point gap between 92.1 and 94.3 look three times as large, and the cut is honest only when zero is not meaningful or the relevant range really is narrow. Panels that look alike may sit on different axes, a linear axis shows size of gains where a log axis shows ratios, and an axis run backwards turns a rise into a fall. Truncation amplifies an effect; inversion reverses it.

Simulation (seeded)

Axes and summaries

What a summary, a window or a missing comparison hides

Source: Chapter 10, “The deceptive visualization catalog”; Exercises 2 and 9; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

A summary, a window or a missing comparison can each be true and still mislead. A median latency conceals a slow tail: about 3 percent of requests reaching around 8 seconds. A trend from April to October shows steady gains, while the full year shows a drop in March and a recovery. And 94 percent accuracy looks like a finding until a baseline appears, since always answering the majority class scores 93 percent on this illustrative data.

Conceptual illustration

Axes and summaries

Look and label

Source: Chapter 10, “The deceptive visualization catalog”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Salience, decoration and wording steer the eye without touching a number. Pale bars for the poor groups recede against dark bars for the good ones; a 3D bar further away draws smaller than an equal bar nearer; and a title that says accuracy boost claims a result before the reader sees data. Name the variables and let the data make the argument.

Conceptual illustration

Documented case

Challenger: the chart that was never drawn

Source: Chapter 10, “Four cases that teach the catalog”; Tufte, Visual Explanations, 1997; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

The night before the Challenger launch, engineers had thirteen charts of O-ring erosion, yet none put temperature and erosion in one view. The pattern that erosion was worse at lower launch temperatures was in the data but never made visible to decision-makers under pressure, and the causal chain from cold to failure was never drawn. Management approved the launch, and seven astronauts died. When a decision is high-stakes and contested, the chart must make the chain visible and the uncertainty too salient to read as inconclusive.

Conceptual illustration

Four cases

Three failures and one success

Source: Chapter 10, “Four cases that teach the catalog”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Four cases teach the catalog. Three are failures: evidence that was never made visible, a tie drawn as a lead, and an inverted axis that drew a rise as a fall. One is a success: Snow's cholera map showed every pump, so the reader could rule out alternatives inside the chart. The Catalonia poll is kept as an illustrative construction because its original graphic could not be found, and Snow's map was drawn after Snow reached his conclusion, so it is a compelling argument rather than the instrument of discovery.

Building honestly

Versions, comparisons and a stranger

Source: Chapter 10, “The Living Deck”; “Snow’s cholera map”; “A working sequence for picking and showing”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Honest building leaves marks a reader can check. A living deck dates every version, puts a changelog second to last, and keeps superseded slides in a previously section, because a polished single version argues that the analysis is finished. An evidence-grade chart shows what could refute the claim, as Snow's map showed every pump. Finally, show the chart to someone who did not build it: if their reading does not match the data, it is not done.

The trade-off

Five choices from honest to misleading

Source: Chapter 10, “The trade-off, stated plainly”; Glimmer 10.1; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

A misleading chart is faster to build and more persuasive on first contact. From one honest chart, five choices of about thirty seconds each turn it into a reassuring one: cut the axes, gray the poor subgroups, drop the intervals, put a bold headline on top, and move calibration into a tab. No number changes. Building both versions on purpose, and naming which choices did the work, shows what default dashboards have been doing without intent.

Conceptual illustration

Working sequence

A working sequence of seven steps

Source: Chapter 10, “A working sequence for picking and showing”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Seven steps turn the ideas into one pass: state the question, locate its family, check that the data measures it, name the sampling uncertainty, name the epistemic uncertainty and where it is disclosed, encode with the perception hierarchy, and test against a hostile read. The sequence has forks. At step three a proxy means finding other data, changing the question, or disclosing the proxy prominently; at step seven a reader whose reading mismatches the data means the chart is not done. Most published charts quietly fail at those two forks.

Conceptual illustration

Tell them apart

Look-alike pairs

Source: Chapter 10.

Four look-alike pairs. Aleatoric variation is real spread in the data, while epistemic doubt is about whether the data is right. A truncated axis amplifies a gap and an inverted axis reverses a trend. A bar of one standard deviation shows the spread of individual values, while a 95 percent interval bar shows uncertainty in the mean, here about ±3.92 against ±10 with n 25. An illustration shows only what supports a claim, while an evidence-grade chart also shows what could refute it.

Computed

Vague request, exact instruction

Say it exactly

Source: Chapter 10, Exercises 1, 2, 3, 9.

Vague chart requests get default charts, and exact ones get what was meant. Five requests become instructions with a computed effect: start bars at zero so a 3.0 times look becomes 1.02 times, draw the poll margin so a 0.8-point gap is seen to be a tie, report the slowest 3 percent beside the median, put the panels on one axis, and show the full year including the drop.

Limits

What would change these conclusions

Source: Chapter 10, “What would change my mind”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Three things could change these conclusions. A visualization standard with strong evidence of matching readers' interpretations would weaken the claim that every dashboard is an argument, and none exists. Evidence that lay readers read bands as accurately as dotplots would shift the technique ranking. And automatic detection of deceptive choices is open: truncated axes and inconsistent scales are mechanical, while color asymmetry, prejudging labels, selective uncertainty and cherry-picked windows need judgment.

Checklist

Before a chart ships

Source: Chapter 10, “A working sequence for picking and showing”; review deck “Chapter 10: Visualization Under Validation”.

Six checks before a chart ships: which question and family, whether the channel fits the precision, whether axes start honestly and match, whether the uncertainty is on the chart at weight and labeled, whether the data measures what the title says, and whether a reader who did not build it reaches a different conclusion. Then phrase requests exactly, such as zero baseline, one axis, label the bar. The choices are normative and there is no neutral, so make them on purpose.
