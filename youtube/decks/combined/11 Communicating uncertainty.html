Communicating uncertainty

Terms for matching a claim’s verb and numbers to the evidence behind it

This deck teaches how to match what a report claims to the evidence behind it: first the verb of a claim, then the numbers that show whether a stated confidence is earned. It covers the verb ladder, writing for several readers, the calibration metrics and their blind spots, and the fixes and their limits. Thirty-four terms are defined along the way, and each carries a tag showing whether the course reading explains it.

Conceptual illustration

The question

The verb is a claim

Source: Chapter 11, opening and “The verb taxonomy”; review deck “Chapter 11: Communicating Uncertainty”.

A model can draft a validation paragraph in a second, but whether its verb is licensed by the evidence is a separate judgment that the model does not make. Here the draft says conclude, while one held-out set with no significance test supports only observe, four rungs lower. Most engineering evidence sits between observe and find and most engineering writing says conclude, and that gap is the problem. The fix runs in two directions: calibrate the verbs in a report you write, and downgrade the verbs in an AI claim someone hands you.

The verb ladder

Warranted assertion and the verb ladder

Source: Chapter 11, “The verb taxonomy”.

The same 87% result can be written with conclude, find or observe, and each verb claims a different amount of evidence. One held-out set with no significance test supports only observe. The eight verbs sit on one frozen ladder from hypothesize to prove, so a report never contradicts itself about which is stronger. Observe needs a clean measurement with its conditions stated, and find needs at least replication.

Conceptual illustration

The verb ladder

Where the ladder comes from, and what it flattens

Source: Chapter 11, “The classical move: warranted assertion”; review deck “Chapter 11: Communicating Uncertainty”.

Warranted assertion means claiming something only in proportion to the evidence for it. The IPCC shows the idea working at scale: its calibrated language pins the word likely to 66–100%, in documents that governments contest line by line. The ladder squeezes two axes onto one line, since suggest and indicate measure inferential caution while observe, find and show measure evidential strength. One defended line is more usable than a correct grid, and indicate stays off it.

Conceptual illustration

The verb ladder

Conclude, fluency traps and the audit

Source: Chapter 11, “The verb taxonomy applied to AI output”; Exercise A1.

Conclude is the strongest verb short of proof, and it needs full analysis, alternatives named and rejected, a sensitivity analysis and a finding that survives it. An AI sentence saying an analysis conclusively demonstrates state-of-the-art performance supplies none of these, so its verb is a flag. Downgrading to suggests keeps the facts and lowers the posture, and the same edit can change a decision, because a reader of conclude ships while a reader of suggest asks for another study. Building and auditing are one skill used on two kinds of text.

Practice with the ladder

Five claims against their evidence

Source: Chapter 11, Exercise W1; review deck “Chapter 11: Communicating Uncertainty”.

Five validation sentences are each checked against the evidence stated for them. A conclusion about robustness from one out-of-distribution dataset, a demonstration from three of five benchmarks, and a finding that a failure never occurs all claim more than the evidence gives. For the last, zero failures in 200 queries only bounds the failure rate below about 1.5% at 95% confidence, found by solving (1 − p) to the power 200 equal to 0.05. The replacements shown are one defensible reading of the ladder, not a unique answer.

Writing it down

One document in three layers

Source: Chapter 11, “Two readers, one document”; Exercise A2.

One document can serve every reader through three layers. Layer 1 is a plain-English summary opening each section, Layer 2 holds methods, equations and tables, and Layer 3 holds the reproducibility detail. The example finding is 89% sensitivity overall and 81% for patients over 75, a gap of 0.08. Plain writing is not dumbed down, because jargon is where an uncalibrated verb hides, and one document beats two because two drift apart.

Writing it down

Saying what you do not know

Source: Chapter 11, “Saying you do not know in writing”; “Why solitary review does not work”.

Uncertainty in prose has to be specific. Epistemic uncertainty shrinks with more evidence, as an interval around an accuracy of 0.87 narrows when n grows, while aleatoric uncertainty stays, as the Brier floor of 0.21 does for a true chance of 0.7. A hedge such as further work is needed carries no information, so it is struck and replaced by a named uncertainty, its kind, a size and what would change it. Peers can see the verb mismatches that the writer cannot, which is why the critique follows six specific steps.

Scoring calibration

Calibration and the reliability diagram

Source: Chapter 11, “The reliability diagram”; exam-term list.

Calibration means that when a model says 80%, it is right about 80% of the time, which is different from being accurate. A reliability diagram bins predictions by stated confidence and plots actual accuracy against it, and a perfect model sits on the diagonal. A curve below the diagonal is overconfidence, common in deep networks but not universal, and a curve above it is underconfidence.

Computed

Scoring calibration

The Brier score and its parts

Source: Chapter 11, “The Brier score: a proper scoring rule”; Exercise W4.

The Brier score is the mean squared gap between a predicted probability and the 0 or 1 outcome, so a confident miss costs the most. It is a proper scoring rule, because the expected score is lowest only when the model reports the true probability. It splits into uncertainty minus resolution plus calibration error, here 0.160 − 0.045 + 0.028 = 0.143, which beats always saying 0.20 at 0.160. One number hides which part drives it, so the parts are always reported.

Where scores fail

ECE, bins and the worst bin

Source: Chapter 11, “Expected Calibration Error: binning the predictions”.

Expected Calibration Error bins the predictions and averages the gap between confidence and accuracy, weighted by how many predictions fall in each bin. Its value depends on the number and width of the bins, so an ECE reported without its bin count cannot be reproduced. Maximum Calibration Error reports the single worst bin instead, which matters when the worst case counts more than the average. A low ECE can hide one badly miscalibrated confidence range.

Computed

Where scores fail

Subgroups, Epic and the evidence ladder

Source: Chapter 11, “Subgroup calibration”; “From calibration metrics to trust claims”.

A good global ECE can hide a failing group. Against a global 0.018, three of five subgroups exceed twice that value and the rare-disease group is 5.7 times higher. The Epic Sepsis Model shows the same pattern in a hospital: an outside check at Michigan Medicine measured an AUC of 0.63 against 0.76 to 0.83 reported by its developer, found alerts that were 88% false, and missed 1,709 of 2,552 sepsis cases. Each of six levels of calibration evidence licenses one verb.

Simulation (seeded)

Where scores fail

Temperature scaling and conformal prediction

Source: Chapter 11, “Temperature scaling: the fix and its limits”; “The conformal prediction guarantee”.

Temperature scaling divides the logits by one learned number T before the softmax, which softens the probabilities without changing the predicted class. T is fitted on a held-out calibration set after the weights are fixed, so it repairs one population's numbers, and at a second hospital where the raw logits were already nearly right it overshoots. Conformal prediction returns a set of labels that covers the truth at least 1 − α of the time, but only on average and only while the data stay exchangeable.

Where scores fail

From calibration metrics to a verb

Source: Chapter 11, “From calibration metrics to trust claims”; review deck “Chapter 11: Communicating Uncertainty”.

Every calibration claim in a report should trace to one row of this table, and the verb in the prose should match the row. One evaluation set earns observe, agreeing sets and subgroup checks earn find, and outside validation with a documented match earns show. A prospective study adds demonstrate, and a formal proof of coverage licenses prove only while exchangeability holds, so under distribution shift show is the ceiling.

Where scores fail

What calibration metrics cannot see

Source: Chapter 11, “What calibration metrics cannot see”; review deck “Chapter 11: Communicating Uncertainty”.

Calibration metrics compare stated probabilities with observed frequencies, and three problems sit outside that. A model can be calibrated on a label that is not the thing the deployment needs predicted, which a construct review catches. It can be perfectly calibrated and still useless, as when it predicts the base rate for everyone and cannot rank cases, which AUROC reveals. And it can drift after the evaluation ends, which only monitoring in deployment shows.

Writing it down

A write-up that survives review

Source: Chapter 11, “What survives review”; review deck “Chapter 11: Communicating Uncertainty”.

A write-up that survives review has eight parts: a Layer 1 summary, the method, findings with calibrated verbs, a calibration section, real limitations, what would change the conclusion, an AI use disclosure and a reproducibility appendix. An adversarial reviewer can then find any claim, its evidence, its limits, the AI use and the way to rerun it. The structure is not elegant, but it is legible to the reviewer. Writing it to support the reviewer rather than defeat them makes the validation stronger in practice.

Tell them apart

Look-alike pairs

Source: Chapter 11; docs/Exam terms - compact list.md, row “temperature (sampling)”.

Four pairs look alike and differ. Accurate asks how often a model is right, while calibrated asks whether its stated chance matches that rate. Epistemic uncertainty shrinks with more data and aleatoric does not. ECE is the weighted average bin gap and MCE is the worst one, and temperature scaling is fitted to data to calibrate, unlike a sampling temperature that a user sets to vary generated text.

Vague claim, exact claim

Say it exactly

Source: Chapter 11, all sections; Exercise W4.

Each vague claim gets an exact version that can be checked, with a chart computed from it. We conclude becomes an observation with its conditions, and the ECE is low becomes a number with its bin count. Fine for everyone becomes a subgroup ratio. A vague claim earns a verdict, while an exact claim gets checked.

Recap

What this material established

Source: Chapter 11, “Where this leaves us”; review deck “Chapter 11: Communicating Uncertainty”.

Seven points sum up the material. The verb of a claim must match its evidence, strong verbs in AI text are flags to check, and one document can serve all readers in layers while naming its uncertainty. The calibration numbers ground the verb, aggregates can hide failing groups, fixes such as temperature scaling and conformal prediction have stated limits, and the metrics cannot see a wrong question, an unhelpful model or a deployment that moved.

Conceptual illustration

Limits

What would change this

Source: Chapter 11, “What would change my mind” and “Still puzzling”.

Two developments would change the framing. A tool that checks verbs against the underlying evidence would reduce the taxonomy to a checklist, and a single metric robust to distribution shift with no binning choices would simplify the multi-metric approach. Two problems stay open: no principled rule says what ratio of subgroup to global ECE should halt a deployment, since that is a values question, and audience layering is hard to teach at scale.

Checklist

Before you trust a claim about uncertainty

Source: Chapter 11, “What survives review”.

Six checks apply before accepting a claim about uncertainty: the verb matches the evidence, every strong verb in AI text has been checked, ECE comes with its bin count and MCE, calibration is split by subgroup, the uncertainty is named with what would change it, and the fix was tested on the deployment population. Then the claim is stated exactly, for example observe ECE 0.04 at 10 bins on one test set.
