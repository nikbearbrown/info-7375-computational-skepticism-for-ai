Bias: where it enters and who is responsible

Terms for finding where a bias lives, what a fix can reach, and who owns the node

Bias in an AI system is not one thing. It is a family of problems that enter at different points between the world and a decision, and each responds to a different fix. The question worth learning to ask is where a given bias lives and which intervention has the most leverage on it. Thirty-three terms carry that question, from the statistical definition of bias to who owns the node that matters.

Illustrative data

The case

Three teams, one disparity

Source: Chapter 6, opening parable; review deck “Chapter 6: Bias”.

Three engineering teams get the same assignment: a deployed system produces biased outcomes, and the job is to fix it. The first adds a fairness penalty to the loss and retrains, and the disparity drops a little; the second recollects data for the underrepresented group, and the disparity changes shape. The third leaves the model and the data alone and changes the human review that the outputs feed into, so the disparity drops by an order of magnitude. This constructed parable isolates a pattern that recurs in real mitigation work, and the bar lengths are illustrative.

The central claim

The fix closest to your hands

Source: Chapter 6, opening and “Leverage analysis”; review deck “Chapter 6: Bias”.

The fix nearest to an engineer's hands is the model or the data, because those sit inside the engineer's own house. The review process, the thresholds and the appeals sit in someone else's house, yet they are often where most of the bias-carrying flow passes. Before fixing anything, ask where the bias lives and which intervention has the most leverage on it. Four tools answer that: a precise definition of bias, ten mechanisms of entry, a way of reading a dataset as an artifact, and Pearl's ladder of causal reasoning.

Simulation (seeded)

What bias means

Estimator, bias and unbiased estimator

Source: Chapter 6, “What “bias” actually means”; “Selection bias”; Exam terms - compact list.

An estimator is any recipe that turns data into a number, and bias is how far its average sits from the true value across every dataset that could have been collected. A random sample centres on the true 25% in this illustrative example, while a filtered sample centres near 14%, an offset of about 11 points. Collecting more data shrinks the scatter and leaves that offset exactly where it was.

Computed

What bias means

2.4 million responses, and wrong

Source: Chapter 6, “What “bias” actually means” (Squire 1988, Public Opinion Quarterly 52(1)); review deck “Chapter 6: Bias”.

In 1936 the Literary Digest poll collected about 2.4 million responses and predicted a landslide for Landon, while Roosevelt won in a landslide. Its lists leaned on telephone owners, car owners and magazine subscribers in the depths of the Depression, and non-response made the skew worse. Gallup used roughly 50,000 respondents, drawn better, and called the race correctly. A sample about 48 times larger lost to a better-drawn one, because more data narrows scatter and does not move a systematic offset.

Ten ways in

Ten ways in, one alphabet

Source: Chapter 6, “Ten mechanisms, distinguished”; Exam terms - compact list.

Ten distinct mechanisms can make an estimate differ from the truth. One enters through the query that builds the sample, five through a person's judgment, two at the keyboard and the sampling frame, and one through a faithful record of an unequal world. The tenth, implicit bias, is not a separate entry point but the medium behind several others. Naming the mechanism matters, because each one has a different causal structure and a different fix.

Computed

Ten ways in

Selection bias, sampling bias and weighting

Source: Chapter 6, “Selection bias”; “The two that enter at the keyboard”; Exam terms - compact list.

Selection bias enters through a reasonable query. Keeping only patients with a lab panel inside six hours drops the critical ones, so a population whose true risk is 25% reads about 14%. Sampling bias is the cousin where groups were hard to reach. Inverse probability weighting gives each kept case a weight of one over its chance of being kept and restores 25%, but only when the cause of exclusion was recorded.

Simulation (seeded)

Ten ways in

Confirmation, self-report and publication bias

Source: Chapter 6, “The five that enter through the humans in the loop”; “The two that enter at the keyboard”; Exam terms - compact list.

Three mechanisms let a person's prior leak into the record. Weighting flattering evidence at 1.5 and denting evidence at 0.5, ten fair pieces of evidence still end at about 97% belief instead of 50%. People report voting more than they did, and the error runs one way, so asking more people does not average it out. When only significant studies are published, the pool reads 0.21 against a true 0.15, and a funnel plot shows the empty corner where the small null studies went; trim-and-fill imputes seven studies and recovers only part of the gap, 0.19.

Computed

Ten ways in

Cohen’s kappa, observer and coding bias

Source: Chapter 6, “The five that enter through the humans in the loop”; “Observer bias compounds with data coding bias”; Exam terms - compact list.

Cohen's kappa is agreement between two coders corrected for the agreement that chance alone would give. Two blind coders who agree on 80 of 100 notes score 0.6, and when one coder can see the prior diagnosis the score falls to 0.2, which points to observer bias. If disagreement clusters on particular items instead of particular people, the category itself is underspecified and the manual needs revising.

Illustrative data

Ten ways in

Historical bias and implicit bias

Source: Chapter 6, “Historical bias”; “Implicit bias”; Exam terms - compact list.

Historical bias has no defect in the data. The sample is representative, the labels are accurate, and the model reproduces the promotion records perfectly, so every metric is green. What it learned is a world nobody wanted to reproduce, so its accuracy is the harm. Implicit bias, the unconscious associations of the people in the pipeline, is the medium behind confirmation, observer, coding and publication bias, so its handles are structural: diverse teams, structured decisions and continuous audits.

Illustrative data

Where it lives

Dataset, label and structural bias

Source: Chapter 6, “Three flavors of bias, mapped to the ten types”; “The dataset as epistemic artifact”; Exam terms - compact list.

Dataset bias is a sampling problem: the training set does not represent the people the system is used on. Label bias is a measurement problem: a recidivism model predicts re-arrest, which is crime filtered through policing. Structural bias is a question about the world: data, labels and model are all correct, yet success depended on mentorship and access that were never evenly distributed. A fix aimed at the wrong location moves nothing.

Illustrative data

Where it lives

Rebalancing can make it worse

Source: Chapter 6, “How bias types compound”.

Bias types rarely act alone, and fixing one can worsen another. Rebalancing a sample raises the group's representation, but if the records were generated under discriminatory conditions the model now sees more of that signal, accurately represented. Eight pairs are known to amplify each other, such as observer bias with data-coding bias, and they are derived interactions of the ten mechanisms and not new ones. Drawing the full causal graph shows whether their paths overlap.

Documented case

Which metric

COMPAS: both analyses were right

Source: Chapter 6, “The dataset as epistemic artifact”; ProPublica, “Machine Bias”, 2016, error rates as cited in review deck “Chapter 6: Bias”.

ProPublica found that in Broward County, Black defendants who did not re-offend were labeled higher risk 44.9% of the time against 23.5% for white defendants, while white defendants who did re-offend were labeled lower risk 47.7% of the time against 28.0%. The tool's makers replied that within each score bucket re-offense rates were about the same across groups, so the score was calibrated. Both analyses were correct and measured different things, because when base rates differ, equal error rates and calibration cannot both hold. The label itself is re-arrest, which reflects policing as well as crime.

Computed

Which metric

The fairness impossibility

Source: Chapter 6, “The dataset as epistemic artifact”; Exam terms - compact list.

Calibration parity means a score carries the same meaning in every group, and equal false-positive and false-negative rates mean errors land equally. With base rates of 30% and 50%, holding positive predictive value at 60% forces the ratio of true-positive rate to false-positive rate to 3.5 in one group and 1.5 in the other. Equal error rates would need those ratios to match, so pick any two and the third breaks. Which error to accept is a values claim and not a technical one.

Computed

Causes

Pearl’s ladder: seeing, doing, imagining

Source: Chapter 6, “Pearl’s Ladder — Rungs 1 and 2”; Exam terms - compact list.

Pearl's ladder has three rungs: seeing asks what goes with what, doing asks what happens if X is set to a value, and imagining asks what would have happened to this case had X been different. A 12-point approval gap between two groups is a finding on the first rung, and setting the zip-code proxy to a neutral value and rerunning is a move on the second: the gap falls to 2 points, so most of the disparity flowed through the proxy. The approval model is illustrative.

Illustrative data

Causes

Seeing is not doing

Source: Chapter 6, “Pearl’s Ladder — Rungs 1 and 2”; credit-card example from review deck “Chapter 6: Bias” (illustrative inputs).

Roosters crow before sunrise, so seeing a crow goes with a sunrise, but stopping the rooster does not stop the sun, so the crow is not the cause. A marketing example makes the same point with numbers: emailed customers signed up at 8.6% against 2.2%, a gap of 6.4 points, yet the marketers had emailed engaged customers, who sign up more anyway. A coin-flip test gives 5.1% against 3.6%, a lift of 1.5 points, so most of the observed gap was targeting. The customer counts and rates are illustrative.

Causes and owners

Causal graph, leverage and owner

Source: Chapter 6, “Leverage analysis”; “A limit case: when the leverage is upstream of your pipeline — and who owns it then”; Exam terms - compact list.

A causal graph writes down who causes what, with arrows from cause to effect, and a proxy lets the protected attribute reach the outcome without being an input. List every path from the attribute to the outcome, then ask what each possible fix blocks and what it leaves open. In this illustrative graph, 2, 3 and 45 of 50 points of disparity flow through the sample, the model and the human reviewer. Changing the review blocks 90% of the flow, and the accountable owner is whoever controls that node.

Illustrative data

Causes and owners

Back to the three teams

Source: Chapter 6, “Leverage analysis”; review deck “Chapter 6: Bias”.

Return to the three teams with every path drawn. Changing the loss blocks the path through the model's parameters, which carries 6% of the disparity in this illustration, and rebalancing the data blocks the underrepresentation path, which carries 4%. Changing the human review blocks a path that bypasses the model and carries 90%, so the disparity falls tenfold. The first two fixes were correct on real paths that happened to be low-leverage, and the third came from drawing the whole graph first.

Conceptual illustration

Causes and owners

When the leverage is upstream

Source: Chapter 6, “A limit case: when the leverage is upstream of your pipeline”; Shapira et al., Agents of Chaos, arXiv:2602.20021, Case #6.

Leverage analysis can return an answer outside the team's reach. In a documented case, an agent deployed by one organization reflected its model provider's training-time choices on contested questions, and those choices sit upstream of everything the deploying engineer controls. The options are to switch providers, accept the behavior, or add downstream filtering and validation. Naming the owner is not assigning blame: it names whoever controls the node at the leverage point, whether that is the team, the labelers, the deployer or the provider.

Tell them apart

Look-alike pairs

Source: Chapter 6.

Four pairs of ideas look alike and are not. Selection bias drops people through a filter, while historical bias keeps everyone and records an unfair world. Association watches X and sees what goes with it, while intervention sets X and sees whether Y moves. Calibration parity and equal error rates cannot both hold when base rates differ, and dataset bias concerns the sample while label bias concerns the stand-in measure.

Computed

Vague claim, exact claim

Say it exactly

Source: Chapter 6.

Five vague claims about bias become checkable once they carry a number. A named estimate against its target shows an offset of about 11 points, and more data narrows the scatter without moving that offset. A named fairness metric shows equal error rates still leaving predictive value at 60% against 78%. Setting the zip proxy to a neutral value shrinks a 12-point gap to 2, and blocking the path that carries 45 of 50 points says where the fix belongs and who owns it.

Limits

What would change my mind

Source: Chapter 6, “What would change my mind — and what I am still puzzling about”; review deck “Chapter 6: Bias”.

One finding would force a rewrite: a debiasing algorithm shown to remove structural bias robustly across domains and base-rate regimes, without touching the deployment context. No such demonstration is known. One problem stays open: with only a labeled training set, dataset bias and label bias are causally distinct but look alike in the data, and telling them apart usually needs access to the labeling process, which another team often controls. Both limits are stated openly so the framing is not mistaken for the last word.

Checklist

Before you accept a bias claim or fix

Source: Chapter 6, “Leverage analysis”.

Six checks come before accepting a bias claim or a bias fix. Name the estimate and the target, ask whether who got in depended on what happened to them, ask whether the data faithfully records a world nobody wants to copy, and place the bias in the sample, the labels or the structure. Then ask whether anyone set X or only watched it, and which path carries the most bias and who owns that node. The last move is to say the claim exactly, as a number against a target.
