Here's a parable built to isolate a real pattern, not a case from any filing. A deployed AI system produces biased outcomes reproducibly. Three engineering teams get the same job, fix it. Team one rewrites the loss function. They add a fairness penalty, a term that punishes unequal error rates across protected groups and retrain on the same data. The gap shrinks a little, not enough. Team two fixes the data. They find the underrepresented group run a costly new collection and retrain. The gap changes shape, smaller in some places, larger in others. Team three touches neither the model nor the data. They look at the room the model was deployed into. A human reviewer reads its outputs and the reviewers own patterns amplified the bias. Team three changes the review process and the gap falls by an order of magnitude. Teams one and two weren't wrong. They made real, small reductions on real paths that carried little of the bias. Team three had leverage and it wasn't luck. It came from a way of looking the other teams hadn't used. That way of looking is the subject. The temptation is to fix what's nearest your hands. The leverage is usually somewhere else. So before fixing anything, ask, where does this bias live and which intervention has the most leverage on it? What bias means? In the newspapers, bias means unfair or prejudiced. That meaning is built for moral clarity, not for engineering. In statistics, bias is a property of an estimator, a rule that turns data into a number. The formal meaning gives up the moral punch and buys you something you can act on. It tells you where to intervene. Call the true quantity theta and your estimate theta hat. Bias is the expected value of theta hat minus theta. Zero means unbiased, maybe wrong on any one data set, but not systematically wrong. Non-zero means systematically off in one direction. Here's the consequence that matters most. More data will not fix a biased estimator. It converges with growing confidence to the wrong answer. More data narrows the scatter. It doesn't move the offset. Here is the run behind that picture. Judgment in the loop. Five more mechanisms share a spine. Somewhere a person exercises judgment and their prior leaks into the record. Confirmation bias is a researcher's prior shaping what gets collected, kept and reported. The other bias is that prior inside one measurement, like an annotator who knows the earlier diagnosis. Self-report bias is the subject's own prior. People report voting more and drinking less than they do. Data coding bias enters when judgment squeezes material into categories, and publication bias is the same leak across a whole literature. Significant results get printed, null stay in drawers. The diagnostic where two humans code the same thing is Cohen's Kappa, agreement beyond chance. Kappa collapses when you unblind the annotators, that's direct evidence of observer bias. In a constructed example, it falls from 0.6 to 0.2. Low Kappa on particular items rather than particular people points at data coding instead. The category is underspecified, not the coder. For publication bias, the analog is the funnel plot. A missing lower left corner is the drawer of small null studies made visible. None of these five entered through the model. They entered upstream of the data when a human judged, so the fixes look like organizational practice, pre-registration, blinding, adversarial review. Historical and implicit bias. Now the one that breaks the pattern, in every mechanism so far, something was wrong with the data, a filter, a prior, a slip. Historical bias has none of that. The sample is representative, the labels are accurate. The data is a faithful recording of the world that produced it, and it is still poison. Imagine a hiring model trained on 10 years of promotion records at a company that promoted men into engineering roles at higher rates. It learns the historical outcome given the candidate and learns it well. Every metric comes back green, but the historical outcome isn't the fair outcome. Its accuracy is the harm. So the fixes can't be data quality fixes. There's no defect to repair. They lower the leverage of the historical signal on today's prediction. A less biased proxy for the outcome, a fairness constraint, or waiting recent records over records from more discriminatory periods. Each trades predictive fit for less inheritance, and a person decides how much fit to spend. Implicit bias, the unconscious associations of the people in the pipeline isn't really a tenth peer. It isn't an entry point. It's the medium several of them swim in, an underground water table, with confirmation, observer, coding, and publication bias as the springs where it reaches daylight. Because it has no single point, it resists a point fix. One handle is a downstream audit, compared the rate of positive predictions across groups. But read a gap as a flag, not a verdict, since real differences in base rates produce the same gap. The fixes with leverage are structural, diverse teams, structured decisions, continuous auditing. Three flavors and how they compound. When people say the model is biased, they can mean three different things. Data set bias, the training set doesn't represent the people the system is deployed on. A face system trained mostly on light skinned faces, then used on everyone, is the textbook example. The bias is in the sample. Label bias, the labels encode a biased process. A recidivism predictor trained on arrest data isn't predicting who commits crimes. It's predicting who gets re-arrested. Those are different variables, and re-arrest is a biased measurement of the thing we care about. Structural bias is the hard case. The data is fine, the labels are fine, the model does its job and the outcomes are still unfair. A hiring model perfectly predicts who succeeds a company today, but success depends on mentorship, sponsorship and interesting projects that were never evenly distributed. The model is correct and its correctness is the problem. Put them side by side, dataset bias lives in the sample, and the fix is to resample or broaden collection. Label bias lives in the labeling step, fix the process or find a better proxy. Structural bias lives in the world the model sits in and no model or data fix reaches it. Treat structural bias as a dataset problem, and you'll spend a quarter and move the disparity nowhere. Bear calls that the most common failure in this field. That's his judgment from experience, not a measured rate. In real systems, several mechanisms run at once. Selection compounds historical bias. There's less data on the underrepresented group, and the data that exists was generated under discriminatory conditions. Rebalancing fixes the first and makes the second worse. You now hold more discriminatory data accurately represented. Observer bias compounds with data coding. Annotator priors become categorical rules baked into the labels. Agreement scores capture the combined effect but can't separate the two. Disagreement clustered on items points to coding. Clustered on annotators, it points to observers. Confirmation compounds with publication. A team designs a study to confirm its hypothesis gets a positive result in publishes while null results from other groups vanish. Trim end fill can correct the publication part. It cannot touch the study design. Bear keeps a short working list of pairs that amplify each other. Selection with historical observer with coding, confirmation with publication, sampling with measurement, aggregation with subgroup masking, evaluation with deployment, annotation with class imbalance, and linkage with re-identification. Once you confirm one, check its partner first. A data set is not the world. A data set is in the world. It's a recording of a slice of it made by particular people at particular time with particular instruments for particular purposes. Each leaves a fingerprint. To use a data set responsibly, read it as a historian reads an archive, what was collected, what was excluded, who decided, and why. Take Compaz, a risk assessment tool used in Broward County, Florida. In 2016, pro-publica analyzed scores for people arrested there in 2013 and 2014. Among defendants who did not re-offend, 44.9% of black defendants had been labeled higher risk against 23.5% of white defendants. And among defendants who did re-offend, 47.7% of white defendants had been labeled lower risk against 28% of black defendants. As pro-publica reported them, the errors fell unevenly in opposite directions. The tools maker North Point answered with a different analysis. Within each risk score, they said about the same share of people re-offended in both groups. In their terms, the score was equally accurate for everyone, predictive parity. There's something deeper in the data. The label wasn't whether this person committed another crime. It was whether this person was re-arrested. Re-arrest depends on crime and on policing. If policing is uneven, re-arrest is an uneven measurement of crime, and every model trained on it inherits the unevenness. The modeler thinks they're predicting recidivism. They're actually predicting re-arrest, given recidivism, given policing, given everything that shapes both. Deployed without that frame, the model launders the unevenness through an algorithm and makes it invisible. Two fairness claims both correct. Both analyzes were correct. They were measuring different things. When two groups have different base rates, no score can satisfy both. Claimberg, Malaynathin and Raghevan proved it in a 2016 paper, and Chittichova showed the same incompatibility for recidivism tools. Here's the plain version. Suppose Group 1 has a true re-offence rate of 30% and Group 250. Make the score calibrated so a given score means the same in both. Now to equalize false positives, you'd have to put the threshold in a different place for each group, which is treating people differently by group. Here's the algebra. For a calibrated score, the odds of being right when flagged equal the base rate odds times the ratio of the true positive rate to the false positive rate. Hold the first equal with different base rates and the ratio of the rates has to differ between groups. Run the numbers, fix the accuracy of a flag at 60% and the true positive rate at 70% for both groups. Then the false positive rate is forced, 20% in Group 1 and 46.7 in Group 2. That gap isn't a bug, it's arithmetic. The three criteria are calibration, equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates. With different base rates, they cannot all hold it once unless prediction is perfect. So the choice between fairness metrics isn't a technical choice. It's a values claim, which kind of error are we less willing to make? Who bears the cost of each error, what the model is used for and the politics of the deployment settle that choice? To tell a system that merely correlates a bias from one that causes it, we need a tool from Judy Pearl, a ladder of causal reasoning with three rungs. We'll work the first two. Rung one is association. What's the probability of why, given that we observe X? It's what most machine learning lives on. It's what calibration curves describe and what most fairness metrics measure. It's also where most thinking about deployed AI quietly stops. Rung two is intervention. The probability of why if we set X to a value? Pearl writes it with do. The notation does real work. Observing X can be tangled up with everything that moves with X, making X happens severs those tangles. The classic illustration is the rooster and the sun. Watch roosters and sunrises, and the sun is very likely up after a crow. But prevent the rooster from crowing and the sunrises anyway. Observation said they were associated. Intervention said the rooster isn't the cause. Rung three is counterfactual. What would have happened to this specific case if X had been different holding everything else fixed? It's the hardest to reach from data alone. Here it just sits at the top of the ladder, visible, not yet climbed. For our problem, the probability of lone denial, given race equals X is the observational quantity. It's what the model sees in training data. The probability of denial if we set race to X, holding everything else fixed is interventional. It asks whether the decision would change if we changed only that variable. The two can come apart. A model can show no disparity on rung one while hiding one on rung two if you intervene on race-correlated features. Or it can show a rung one disparity that vanishes on rung two because it runs entirely through legitimate features. Without telling them apart, you can't tell a fair model from one that's merely been smoothed. Most deployed mitigation works on rung one. Adjust the conditional distributions until the metric reads the way the engineer wants. That's sometimes useful and rarely sufficient, because whether a variable causes the bias is a rung two question and rung one can't answer it. Here are the same audit questions asked at each rung. For lone denial, is the rate equal across groups? Or would it change if race changed and nothing else did? For hiring is the higher rate proportional or would two identical applicants be treated differently? Finding the leverage.
