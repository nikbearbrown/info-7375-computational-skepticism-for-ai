## The content (the chapter's arc; one beat per step, cut anything that repeats)
Frame: verification is the problem; what does the human actually do? Five capacities, each with the chapter's own failure picture. Keep one cast across the film (the SKILL's "same objects, whole film" law): a model box, an output page, a supervisor figure, the world (a patient / a store of record), so each capacity is the supervisor doing one distinct thing with the same objects.
1. **Plausibility auditing.** The twitch: a clinician looks at a low-acuity score for a 49-year-old with a swollen leg and something twitches. The check is against the ambient prior built over years. The model has its training data; the supervisor has the world. Failure: nobody asks whether the output makes sense on its face.
2. **Problem formulation.** Most AI failures are failures of formulation, not performance. The triage system answered "what statistical category is this presentation?"; the clinical question was "how likely is a life-threatening condition?" Two questions; the first answered well, the second never asked. Failure: the model answers a different question than the one that matters.
3. **Tool orchestration.** AI is one tool among many; the supervisor decides AI / human judgment / a different model / **no tool yet**. "We should not deploy this here" is supervisory work, often the most valuable. Failure: AI used because it's available; the "no tool yet" decision never made because nobody has the mandate.
4. **Interpretive judgment.** The same number means different things in different deployments: 0.7 confidence high in one domain, disqualifying in another; a 5% false-positive rate fine for a spam filter, catastrophic for a cancer screen. The number is not the meaning; the meaning is the number-in-the-domain. Failure: numbers read off a dashboard with no domain translation.
5. **Executive integration.** Outputs from many tools, models and people synthesized into a decision someone stands behind: where the buck stops. The rarest capacity, most often missing, because no model produces it and most pipelines have no place for it. Failure: the pipeline produces decisions nobody owns; accountability dissolves into the architecture.
6. **The close.** By the end of the book you can name, for every step of a deployment, which capacity is exercised and by whom. Where you can't name it, you've found a gap. "Gaps are where the patients die." Draw the five as a row with one slot empty.
7. **Your Turn idea:** pick one AI deployment you know (or one step of one). For each of the five capacities, write who exercises it, in one line, or write "nobody." Two checks: is every "who" a named role, not "the team"? Did you write "nobody" at least once, honestly?
